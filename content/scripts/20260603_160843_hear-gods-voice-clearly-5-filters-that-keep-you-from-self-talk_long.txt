If you’ve ever asked, “Is this God or just my thoughts?” this is for you. Think it’s God? Run it through these five filters. In a few minutes, you’ll have a simple, biblical way to move from confusion to clarity without overthinking yourself into a corner.

First, set your expectation. God leads; we test. Scripture says in 1 Thessalonians 5:21, “Test everything; hold fast to what is good.” That’s not doubt. That’s discipleship. God is not threatened when you test a prompting. He invites it, because He is a Father who wants you to know His voice, not guess at it. Jesus said, “My sheep hear my voice” in John 10:27. You don’t have to force a voice. You follow a Shepherd. With that settled, here are five filters to keep you from mistaking self-talk for God’s voice.

Filter one is Scripture alignment. God’s voice will never contradict God’s Word. Second Timothy 3:16 says all Scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting, and training in righteousness. So when a thought comes, ask, “Does this align with the clear teaching and character of God revealed in Scripture?” If a voice tells you to hold on to bitterness, manipulate someone, cut corners, or sin “for a good reason,” that’s not the Spirit of truth. On the other hand, if the thought points you toward honesty, repentance, forgiveness, generosity, purity, and love, it resonates with His Word. Notice the core: the Bible is your boundary line. When it’s gray, stay close to what’s clear, and let the clear guide the unclear.

Filter two is Christlikeness. Jesus said His sheep hear His voice because they know Him. The question becomes, “Does this make me look more like Jesus?” His voice produces humility, obedience, and love. It leads you to serve, not to be seen; to forgive, not to get even; to wash feet, not to win arguments. If a thought inflates your ego or fuels self-importance, be careful. The Spirit magnifies Christ, not our pride. And remember the difference between condemnation and conviction. Condemnation says, “You’re a failure, give up.” Conviction says, “That was wrong, come back.” Jesus never shames you into change; He calls you by name back to Himself.

Filter three is peace and fruit. Colossians 3:15 says, “Let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts.” Think of peace like an umpire—calling safe or out. Galatians 5:22–23 describes the Spirit’s fruit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. God’s voice doesn’t always lead to easy circumstances, but it does lead to inner clarity and a growing harvest of good fruit over time. If a thought creates panic, manipulation, frantic urgency, or leaves a bitter aftertaste, slow down. If, even in a hard step, there is a settled trust in God and a direction toward patience and kindness, pay attention. Fruit doesn’t lie.

Filter four is wise counsel. Proverbs 11:14 says, “In an abundance of counselors there is safety.” God often confirms His leading through mature believers who know Scripture and know you. Seek counsel from people who aren’t trying to impress you, rescue you, or rubber-stamp your plan. Ask them to push back, to pray, to ask hard questions. Wise counsel is not a replacement for God’s voice; it is a safeguard against your blind spots. Pride isolates; wisdom invites input.

Filter five is providence and open doors. Revelation 3:8 records Jesus saying, “I have set before you an open door, which no one is able to shut.” God is sovereign over timing and opportunities. He can open doors you can’t pry and close doors you can’t force. When conditions align—resources, timing, invitations, needs—it may be a sign of His leading. But remember, an open door alone is not a yes, and a closed door is not always a no. Run the door through the other filters. Don’t manipulate outcomes to make them look like doors; trust the Doorkeeper.

Let’s bring it together with a quick case study. Imagine Maria, who has felt restless at work. She prays and thinks she hears, “Quit your job this week.” She’s tired, overwhelmed, and unsure. How does she test it?

She starts with Scripture. God values work, stewardship, and caring for needs. Nothing in Scripture says impulsiveness equals faith, and 2 Timothy 3:16 reminds her the Word corrects and trains. Quitting might be part of a longer obedience, but Scripture won’t support abandoning commitments out of frustration. That nudges her to slow down.

She looks at Christlikeness. Does leaving now make her more like Jesus, or is it escape? Is the thought pushing her to love others well, finish with integrity, and trust God, or to vent and bail? She senses a mix: a holy longing to serve elsewhere and an unholy urge to run. That’s a yellow light.

She checks peace and fruit. In prayer, she doesn’t feel settled about “this week,” but she does sense peace about exploring a transition with integrity. The fruit of patience and self-control seem to rise when she imagines a planned exit, while panic spikes when she imagines a sudden resignation. Another caution.

Next, she seeks wise counsel. She talks with a mentor and a small group leader who pray, ask questions, and suggest a timeline: update her resume, have an honest conversation with her boss, and apply to roles that match her calling. They don’t just agree; they help her discern.

Finally, she watches providence. Within a month, a role opens at a nonprofit that fits her gifts. Interviews go well. The door begins to open, not because she forced it, but because God did. When she runs this open door through the other filters again, they align: it doesn’t contradict Scripture, it grows Christlike character, peace rules even with some nerves, and counsel affirms. She moves forward, not from panic, but from peace.

Here’s a 30-second practice you can use anytime. Get quiet, even in a busy place. Breathe in and pray, “Speak, Lord, your servant is listening.” Name the impression you’re testing in one sentence. Then move through the filters with quick yes or no responses: Does this align with Scripture? Does it look like Jesus? Is peace ruling and fruit growing? What do my wise counselors say? Are doors opening without me forcing them? If any answer raises a red flag, pause. If they align, take the next small obedient step and keep listening.

Two reminders for your heart. First, timing is part of God’s voice. Sometimes the message is yes, but not yet. Waiting on the Lord is not punishment; it’s preparation. Isaiah says those who wait on the Lord renew their strength. Second, failure doesn’t disqualify you. If you’ve misheard before, don’t let shame shut your ears. Grace means God can correct your steps without condemning your soul. He’s a better Shepherd than you are a sheep.

If God feels silent, stay near the places He has already spoken: His Word, His people, His peace, His providence. Keep short accounts with God. Confess quickly. Obey the last clear thing He said. Often, clarity meets us in the middle of simple faithfulness.

So the next time you wonder, “Is this God or just me?” remember this path. Test everything; hold fast to what is good. Start with Scripture. Look for Christlikeness. Let peace and fruit rule. Invite wise counsel. Watch for God’s doors. Over time, you’ll grow fluent in the Shepherd’s tone and quieter to the noise of self-talk and fear.

For a quick reminder, hold on to these five words: Scripture, Christlikeness, Peace and Fruit, Counsel, Open Doors. Let them live in your journal, your prayers, your decisions. God is not playing hide and seek with His will; He’s leading, and you can learn to recognize it.
